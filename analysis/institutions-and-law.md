# Institutions and the law: dogs on campus

_Desk research, compiled 24 November 2026. This is not fieldwork. Nothing here is an IIT Roorkee count, quote, or hospital figure. News reports are marked as news. A claim with no document behind it is left out. The group's own findings stay in [`synthesis.md`](synthesis.md)._

The short version: MIT, Stanford and Harvard do not run a street-dog programme. They ban or tightly license owned pets, allow trained service dogs, and sit inside city leash laws that impound a dog found loose. The campuses that face the same problem as IIT Roorkee are other Indian residential campuses. Since 7 November 2025 the Supreme Court has split Indian law in two: ordinary streets still use sterilise-and-return; educational institutions are to be fenced, and dogs removed from them are not to be put back in the same place.

## 1. What the foreign universities actually do

These three do not have a free-roaming campus dog population to manage. Their rules are about animals that belong to someone.

**MIT.** Housing policy prohibits pets in any undergraduate or graduate residence. A non-resident may not enter a residence hall with an animal, except a service animal trained to do work for a person with a disability. An assistance animal (often an emotional-support animal) is allowed only if Disability and Access Services approves it as a housing accommodation. ([Animals in Residence Halls](https://studentlife.mit.edu/policies-and-resources/animals-in-residence-halls/))

In CSAIL, a pet in a public area must be on a leash or in a crate, and each person who shares a room can veto it. ([CSAIL Pet Guidelines](https://www.csail.mit.edu/csail-pet-guidelines))

MIT's environment, health and safety rules for animal events say animals enter MIT spaces in a crate or on a leash or harness, stay under the handler's control, and that off-leash or free-roaming activity is not permitted. That page is about events and therapy-dog visits, not a campus pack. ([MIT EHS, animal-event safety](https://ehs.mit.edu/?post_type=basic-page-new))

**The city MIT sits in.** Cambridge, Massachusetts, requires every dog six months or older to be licensed, and proof of spay or neuter is part of licensing. A dog off the owner's premises must be on a chain, rope or cord no longer than six feet, unless it is inside a signed off-leash area with the keeper present and a leash in hand. A dog found at large is caught and held. The owner has seven days to recover it and pays the cost of care. ([Cambridge Code of Ordinances, Title 6](https://library.municode.com/ma/cambridge/codes/code_of_ordinances?nodeId=TIT6AN))

Massachusetts requires the owner of a dog, cat or ferret aged six months or older to have it vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian, and revaccinated on the manufacturer's schedule. The fine for a violation is not more than $100. Research-institution animals are exempt from that section. ([Massachusetts animal laws, 2025 edition, G.L. c. 140 § 145B](https://www.mass.gov/doc/animal-laws-and-regulations-in-massachusetts-2025-edition/download))

**Stanford.** The university follows the Americans with Disabilities Act for service animals: a dog individually trained to perform a task for a person with a disability. A dog whose only role is comfort or emotional support is not a service animal. Service animals may be in public campus spaces. Support animals generally may not be in classrooms or cafeterias. A service animal must be harnessed, leashed or tethered unless the disability prevents it or the device interferes with the task. Stanford may remove an animal that is a direct threat, and may report an animal to city and county animal control. ([Stanford Diversity and Access Office](https://diversityandaccess.stanford.edu/disability-access/Stanford-Visitor-information-service-and-support-animals))

In student housing for 2025–26, a support animal needs prior approval from the Office of Accessible Education and registration with Residential and Dining Enterprises. It is allowed in the assigned residence, not across campus. It may not be left overnight unattended. The owner is liable for injury and damage and must follow local leash laws. ([Stanford R&DE residence agreement, 2025–26](https://rde.stanford.edu/studenthousing/residence-agreement-policies-and-procedures-2025-26))

**The city Stanford sits in.** Palo Alto Municipal Code § 6.16.100: no one may allow a dog, licensed or not, on a public street, sidewalk, park, school ground, public place, or unenclosed lot unless it is held on a leash by an able-bodied person. A violation is an infraction. City park rules say the same: leashed everywhere except a dedicated dog park. A Palo Alto dog licence requires proof of rabies vaccination. ([Palo Alto § 6.16.100](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/paloalto/latest/paloalto_ca/0-0-0-65554); [Palo Alto dog parks](https://www.paloalto.gov/Departments/Public-Works/Engineering-Services/Engineering-Projects/City-of-Palo-Alto-Dog-Parks); [Palo Alto dog licensing](https://www.paloalto.gov/Departments/Police/Animal-Control/Dog-Licensing))

**Harvard**, as the third institution. Harvard College residences: no animal in a College building except an approved service or assistance animal. ([Harvard College residential life](https://dso.college.harvard.edu/policies/residential-life))

Harvard University Housing, 2026–27 leasing manual: dogs only in designated pet-friendly apartments; one dog, no larger than 40 pounds full grown; some breeds barred; current rabies vaccination and a yearly licence; veterinary records before move-in and again before a lease extension; animal guests and pet-sitting not permitted; an unauthorised animal is grounds for ending the lease. A service animal is defined there as a dog trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. ([Harvard University Housing policies, 2026–27](https://www.huhousing.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/TenantPolicyManual%202627.pdf))

**What this model is.** The dog has an owner. The owner vaccinates, licenses, and leashes it. A loose dog is impounded, not sterilised and returned to a territory. There is no feeder registration and no campus feeding zone, because there is no ownerless population these policies are written for.

## 2. A university that does keep free-roaming dogs

**Boğaziçi University, Istanbul**, is the documented case found in this pass. Its own flora-and-fauna note, covering December 2020 to February 2023, says:

- 153 cats were microchipped, with parasite treatment and vaccines continuing. Cat houses and food and water bowls were placed on the north and south campuses.
- 22 dogs received parasite treatment and preventive vaccines. Sterilisation of cats and dogs was continuing. A university veterinarian handles illness and injury, with the municipality or private clinics when needed.
- Dogs observed to be aggressive are sent to animal living areas.
- Dogs are trained so that they do not attack visually impaired students because of white canes.
- Food goes in the bowls already provided. Raw meat and bones are forbidden, on the stated ground that raw meat makes dogs aggressive toward cats, people and vehicles.
- Animals from outside are not to be left on campus. The note says the population is already too large and sterilisation is expensive.
- Cats are not to be fed inside buildings.

([Boğaziçi University, Flora / Fauna](https://bogazici.edu.tr/tr/pages/flora-fauna/134))

This is the nearest "managed coexistence" document from a university outside India: vaccinate, sterilise, do not import new animals, do not feed inside buildings, and remove the aggressive individuals. The counts are the university's own, for that date range only.

**National law behind it.** Turkey's Animal Protection Law, No. 5199 (accepted 24 June 2004), as amended by Law No. 7527 of 30 July 2024, on the official legislation site:

- Article 6: ownerless or weakened animals may not be killed, except for exceptions in law.
- The same article, last sentence substituted in 2024: animals taken into a shelter are entered in the Ministry's data system, and rehabilitated dogs are kept in the shelter until they are adopted.
- Article 13, retitled "Euthanasia of animals" in 2024: for dogs taken into a shelter that endanger human or animal life and health and whose negative behaviour cannot be controlled, or that have a contagious or untreatable disease, or whose adoption is prohibited, the measure in Article 9(3) of the Veterinary Services Law, No. 5996, is applied. This note does not paraphrase what Article 9(3) of Law 5996 itself says, because that section was not retrieved.
- Abandoning a collected animal outside a shelter, or releasing a shelter dog outside the shelter, is listed as a prohibited act.
- Metropolitan municipalities, provincial municipalities, and municipalities with a population over 25,000 must set aside a specified resource. A mayor or official who does not, or who spends it on something else, faces imprisonment of six months to two years.

([Law No. 5199, mevzuat.gov.tr](https://www.mevzuat.gov.tr/MevzuatMetin/1.5.5199.htm))

The 2024 amendment moved Turkey away from "sterilise and leave on the street" for dogs: the statute now says rehabilitated dogs stay in the shelter until adoption, and it opens a euthanasia article for a defined subset. It still begins from a ban on killing, with legal exceptions.

## 3. England, and the international health standard

**England and Wales.** A stray dog is any dog in a public or private place without a responsible person, including a deliberately abandoned dog. The local authority alone must deal with it. A welfare charity has no legal duty to do so. On a report, the authority collects the dog if it is in a public place or on private land. Sections 149 to 151 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990 do not give a power to enter private premises; the occupier's permission is required. The dog is scanned for a microchip, checked against the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991, and held for seven full days starting the day after collection, with food, water, kennelling and veterinary care. The statutes named by the government guidance are the Clean Neighbourhoods and Environment Act 2005 (section 68), the Environmental Protection Act 1990, the Environmental Protection (Stray Dogs) Regulations 1992, and the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991. ([GOV.UK, stray dogs](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/stray-and-abandoned-dogs-local-authority-responsibilities))

**Oxford** is the city, not a university policy. Oxford City Council's Public Spaces Protection Order, in force from 19 December 2023 for three years, requires a person in charge of a dog to put it on a lead when a police officer or authorised person directs it, where that is reasonably necessary to prevent a nuisance. It also requires immediate removal of faeces, limits one person to four dogs, and excludes dogs from signed, fenced children's play areas. ([Oxford City Council PSPO, 2023](https://www.oxford.gov.uk/downloads/file/2203/dog-control-pspo-2023))

**WHO.** Dog bites cause almost all human rabies cases. Vaccinating at least 70% of dogs in an at-risk area, in recurrent mass campaigns, is the coverage WHO says should maintain herd immunity and break dog-to-dog transmission. Human post-exposure vaccination prevents deaths but does not eliminate the disease. If the biting animal is in a rabies-endemic country, medical advice is sought immediately. Post-exposure prophylaxis is required when the dog is rabid or suspected, or of unknown vaccine status; a clinician decides. ([WHO rabies FAQ, 14 February 2018](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/ntds/rabies/rabies-general-public-faqs.pdf); [WHO, vaccinating against rabies](https://www.who.int/activities/vaccinating-against-rabies-to-save-lives))

### What the Indian campus documents have in common

Where a written policy exists (Bombay 2024 and 2026, Kanpur 2025), it does four things: named feeding spots away from academic buildings and hostels; a ban on feeding elsewhere, with a sanction; a sterilisation and vaccination arrangement; and a boundary around the places dogs may not be. None of these documents is a removal of every dog from the whole campus. Bombay's April 2026 note is explicit that it is interim, pending the municipality, and it cites the Supreme Court order below.

## 5. Indian law

Parent statute: the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960. The Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023, were notified under it on 10 March 2023 as G.S.R. 193(E), superseding the Animal Birth Control (Dogs) Rules, 2001. The government press note says the rules follow Supreme Court directions in Writ Petition No. 691 of 2009, *Animal Welfare Board of India v. People for Elimination of Stray Troubles*, and that those directions did not permit relocation. ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=1917510&lang=2&reg=48); rules text used here: [ABC Rules, 2023, Tamil Nadu Animal Welfare Board PDF](https://tnawb.tn.gov.in/pdf/ANIMAL_BIRTH_CONTROL_2023.pdf))

That "no relocation" sentence is the 2023 government's description of the older case. The 2025 and 2026 orders below qualify it for institutional premises. Both have to be read.

### What the 2023 Rules still say

- **Rule 7(2).** One class of animal is "street dogs or community owned Indian dogs or abandoned pedigreed dogs which are homeless, living on the street or within a gated campus."
- **Rule 8(2).** For street animals, the local authority is responsible for deworming, immunisation and sterilisation. It may engage a Board-recognised animal welfare organisation.
- **Rule 5.** No street-dog birth-control programme without a certificate of project recognition from the Animal Welfare Board.
- **Rule 11(5), (8) and (19).** Capture by humane methods. Tongs and wires are prohibited. Each dog gets a numbered collar so that it is released in the same area it came from. Rule 11(19), as set out in the Supreme Court judgment of 19 May 2026: "The dogs shall be released at the same place or locality from where they were captured and the date, time and place of their release shall be recorded."
- **Rule 15.** Euthanasia is for incurably ill and mortally wounded dogs, diagnosed by a team of the jurisdictional veterinary officer, the project in-charge, and a Board or State Board representative, by a qualified veterinarian, using intravenous sodium pentobarbital or another approved humane method. Not in the presence of another dog. Reasons recorded. The rule text retrieved does not authorise euthanasia for aggression.
- **Rule 16.** Bite and rabies complaints are logged. A bite report goes promptly to a government hospital. The dog is humanely captured and observed. A suspected rabid dog is inspected by a panel (a veterinary surgeon appointed by the local authority, and a representative of an animal welfare organisation). If rabies is highly probable, the dog is isolated until natural death, which the rule says normally occurs within ten days. If the dog does not have rabies but has another disease or is "furious in nature", it is handed to an animal welfare organisation to be treated and released after ten days of observation.
- **Rule 20.** The residents' welfare association, apartment association, or local-body representative arranges feeding with the people who already feed. Feeding spots are agreed, matched to territories, and kept away from children's play areas, entries and exits, and staircases. Feeding times avoid the movement of children and older people. A dispute goes to an animal welfare committee (veterinary officer, police, SPCA or State Board, the ABC organisation, the local veterinary officer, the complainant, and the association). That committee's decision on the feeding point is final, with an appeal to the State Board.

([ABC Rules PDF](https://tnawb.tn.gov.in/pdf/ANIMAL_BIRTH_CONTROL_2023.pdf); Rule 11(19) quotation from [2026 INSC 506](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/41706/41706_2025_2_1501_71480_Judgement_19-May-2026.pdf))

### The Supreme Court, Suo Motu Writ Petition (Civil) No. 5 of 2025

*In Re: "City Hounded by Strays, Kids Pay Price".* One case, four dates that matter. Bench for the orders below, unless noted: Justices Vikram Nath, Sandeep Mehta and N.V. Anjaria.

**11 August 2025, two-judge order, Delhi NCR only**, as recited in the later judgment. Authorities in Delhi, Ghaziabad, Noida, Faridabad and Gurugram were to pick up stray dogs from all localities, move them to shelters, sterilise, deworm and immunise them, and in no circumstances release them back to the streets. Obstruction was to be treated as contempt. ([2025 INSC 1018, 22 August 2025](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/41706/41706_2025_3_1501_63567_Judgement_22-Aug-2025.pdf), quoting the 11 August order)

**22 August 2025, 2025 INSC 1018.** The three-judge bench modified that order. Paragraphs 12(i) and 12(ii) of 11 August continued. The ban on release was kept in abeyance. Dogs picked up are sterilised, dewormed, vaccinated, and released back to the same area, except dogs infected or suspected to be infected with rabies, and dogs that display aggressive behaviour. Those are not released back to the streets, and are kept in a separate pound or shelter as far as possible. The judgment gives the reason for return: Rule 11(19), shelter capacity, and returning a treated dog to the place it already lived. It also says a blanket impound of every dog in the NCR was not practical without the infrastructure. ([2025 INSC 1018](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/41706/41706_2025_3_1501_63567_Judgement_22-Aug-2025.pdf))

**7 November 2025, 2025 INSC 1357.** This is the order about campuses. It is pan-India, not only the NCR. The Court names educational institutions, hospitals, sports complexes, bus stands and depots, and railway stations. It records, as a fact it was informed of, that the campus of the National Law School of India University, Bengaluru, has a large stray population that threatens students, staff and faculty. Causes it lists: uncontrolled breeding from weak sterilisation, food waste, weak perimeters and weak coordination with the municipality, and weak public knowledge of how to behave and what to do after a bite.

Operative directions:

- **A.** States and Union Territories, through local bodies, identify all government and private educational institutions, hospitals, public sports complexes, bus stands and depots, and railway stations, within two weeks.
- **B.** The administrative head, through the local body and under the District Magistrate, secures the premises with fencing, boundary walls, gates, or other measures so that stray dogs cannot enter. Preferably within eight weeks.
- **C.** Each such institution designates a nodal officer for cleanliness and for ensuring that stray dogs do not enter or live on the premises. The name is displayed at the entrance and notified to the municipality.
- **D.** Municipalities and panchayats inspect at least once every three months. A lapse is fixed on the officials.
- **E.** The municipal body removes every stray dog found on those premises to a designated shelter, after sterilisation and vaccination under the ABC Rules. The dog is not released back to the same location. The Court says return would defeat the purpose of clearing those places.
- **F.** Government and private hospitals keep anti-rabies vaccine and immunoglobulin in stock.
- **G.** The Ministry of Education directs every school and educational institution to hold awareness sessions on behaviour around animals, bite first aid, and reporting.
- **J.** The Animal Welfare Board issues SOPs within four weeks. The SOP dated 27 November 2025 is the document the later judgment upholds.

The order says these directions continue the order of 22 August 2025, and that they are to comply with the principles in the ABC Rules. Non-compliance may lead to suo motu contempt. Central institutions, including those under the Ministry of Education, are expressly covered. Listed next for 13 January 2026. ([2025 INSC 1357, 7 November 2025](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/41706/41706_2025_3_301_65687_FinalOrder_07-Nov-2025.pdf))

**19 May 2026, 2026 INSC 506.** Applications asking the Court to modify, recall or stay the 7 November directions were dismissed. Holdings used here, from the judgment:

- The ABC Rules regulate population. They do not give stray dogs an absolute right to remain in every kind of place. Rule 7(2) classifies animals. It does not create a right to occupy a gated campus indefinitely.
- Rule 11(19)'s "same place or locality" is read with section 2(i) of the PCA Act, which defines "street" as a way, road, lane, square, court, alley, passage or open space to which the public have access. The Court holds that this does not extend the release duty to institutional or controlled-access premises.
- Dogs inside educational institutions, hospitals, sports complexes, airports, bus stands and railway stations are held not to get the benefit of release-back under Rule 11(19). Direction E of 7 November 2025 is affirmed.
- Direction H of 19 May 2026: where the stray population is alarming and bites or aggressive attacks are frequent and a continuing threat, authorities may take measures that are legally permissible, including euthanasia of rabid, incurably ill, or demonstrably dangerous or aggressive dogs, after assessment by qualified veterinary experts, and strictly in accordance with the PCA Act, the ABC Rules, and other statutory protocols. This does not replace Rule 15. Rule 15, as retrieved, still limits euthanasia to incurably ill and mortally wounded dogs. The judgment itself says the measure must stay inside those rules. An aggressive dog is not, on this text alone, a dog that may be killed.
- Direction I: officers implementing these orders in good faith, including heads of schools, colleges and hospitals, are not ordinarily to face an FIR for that implementation, except where there is a prima facie case of mala fides, gross abuse, or action wholly outside the Court's directions.
- At least one working ABC centre in every district, with expansion where density requires it. States decide, by a reasoned decision, whether to extend the institutional directions to other high-footfall public places.
- Every High Court registers a suo motu case titled *In Re: Compliance with the directions issued by the Supreme Court in Suo Motu Writ Petition (Civil) No(s). 5 of 2025*, before a Division Bench, as a continuing mandamus. High Courts may tailor directions to local conditions without diluting them, and may initiate contempt. Chief Secretaries file compliance affidavits in the High Court by 7 August 2026. The matter was listed in the Supreme Court on 17 November 2026.

([2026 INSC 506, 19 May 2026](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/41706/41706_2025_2_1501_71480_Judgement_19-May-2026.pdf))

**17 November 2026.** A PTI report of that day's hearing says the bench pointed out that collared dogs were still on the premises of the Gujarat and Delhi High Courts, noted compliance reports from 19 states, described Delhi's measures as inadequate and Assam and Bihar as needing improvement, reserved its order on compliance, told intervenors to go to the High Courts, and posted the matter for January 2027. The same PTI copy says the 19 May verdict "allowed euthanasia" of rabid, incurably ill, dangerous and aggressive dogs. The judgment's own direction H is narrower: only where bites are frequent and a continuing threat, only after a veterinary assessment, and only within the PCA Act and the ABC Rules. Use the judgment, not the wire paraphrase.

### The two regimes, side by side

| Place | What the law requires | Release back? |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary streets and localities | Local authority captures, sterilises, vaccinates, deworms. Rule 11(19) and the order of 22 August 2025. | Yes, to the same area. Not if rabid or suspected rabid, and not if the dog displays aggressive behaviour: those stay in a shelter. A "furious" dog that is not rabid is, under Rule 16(6), treated and released after ten days. |
| Educational institutions, hospitals, sports complexes, bus stands, railway stations | Municipality removes every stray dog found there to a designated shelter, after sterilisation and vaccination. Institution fences the premises and names a nodal officer. Inspections at least quarterly. | No. Not back to that institution. Affirmed 19 May 2026. |
| Feeding | Rule 20 still requires agreed spots, away from children, entries and stairs, for community animals in a residential area. The May 2026 compliance format also asks states to report dedicated feeding spaces in each municipal ward. | Feeding spots are not a right to keep dogs inside a hostel or a classroom. |

Who does what on a campus, under the 7 November order: the institute secures the boundary and names the officer. The municipal body removes the dog and shelters it. The institute does not, on these directions, become the ABC authority.

IIT Roorkee is in Uttarakhand, so the continuing mandamus sits in the Uttarakhand High Court. This pass did not retrieve that High Court's order, or any Roorkee municipal compliance affidavit.

## 6. What this does to the group's own recommendations

[`synthesis.md`](synthesis.md) recommends keeping dogs on campus but out of hostels, fewer enforced feeding zones, a repaired boundary, a public sterilisation count, a 24-hour bite route, a report line for a specific aggressive dog, and orientation. The documents above cut that list in three.

**Still aligned with both the campus policies and the statute.** Named feeding spots away from hostels, messes and the academic area, with a sanction for feeding elsewhere (Bombay, Kanpur, Rule 20). Caged or closed bins (Kanpur; also the Court's list of causes on 7 November). A published sterilisation and vaccination record, done by a project that holds Board recognition (Rules 5 and 8; Kanpur's Animal Welfare Cell; Bombay's contracted agency). A written bite route and stocked anti-rabies vaccine (direction F of 7 November; Rule 16(2); WHO on post-exposure prophylaxis). Orientation on how to behave and what to do after a bite (direction G).

**Now a legal duty, not only a campus preference.** A boundary that actually keeps dogs out, and one named nodal officer displayed at the entrance (directions B and C). Removal of a dog found inside the institution is the municipality's job, to a shelter, without return to that spot (direction E, affirmed 19 May 2026). "Repair the wall so outside dogs cannot enter" is the part of the group's R3 that the Court has since required. "Keep the existing campus dogs where they are, once sterilised" is the part direction E does not allow, for dogs inside the institution.

**Do not say.** Do not say the ABC Rules have been repealed. Rule 11(19) still governs streets. Do not say the Court has authorised culling of aggressive dogs: direction H is expressly inside Rule 15 and the rest of the ABC Rules, and Rule 15 as retrieved is limited to incurably ill and mortally wounded dogs. Do not cite MIT, Stanford or Harvard as campuses that solved a street-dog conflict. They never had this one. Do not cite the Times of India figures for JNU, or any sterilisation percentage for IIT Bombay that is not in an institute document. Do not cite the Boğaziçi counts as current; they stop at February 2023.
